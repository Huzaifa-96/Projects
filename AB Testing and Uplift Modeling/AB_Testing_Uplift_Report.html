Plain-English Summary

Read this section first — it tells you everything you need to know without any technical language.

✅ Bottom Line: The email campaign worked — and worked well. Customers who received the personalised discount email were nearly 70% more likely to make a purchase than those who did not. This result is statistically rock-solid and not due to chance. The campaign should be rolled out, but with smart targeting: not all customers respond equally, and sending emails to the wrong people wastes money.

The Question We Asked

Should the retailer invest in sending personalised discount emails to its customer base? And if so, who should receive them? These are not simple questions. Some customers would have bought anyway — sending them a discount just cuts into profit margin. Others simply never respond to emails, no matter what. The goal is to find the persuadable customers: people who genuinely convert because of the email, who would not have done so without it.

What We Did

We ran a randomised controlled trial — the gold standard in scientific testing. Around 5,000 customers were randomly split into two groups. One group received the personalised discount email; the other received nothing. By comparing the two groups, we can measure the true effect of the email with confidence. We then built machine learning models to score each individual customer on how likely they are to respond to the campaign — so future emails can be targeted precisely.

Smarter targeting can dramatically improve the campaign's return on investment.

Section 02

The Business Problem — Why This Matters

The Cost of Indiscriminate Email Marketing

Most email marketing campaigns are sent to entire customer lists without considering whether individual customers will actually respond. This approach has a hidden cost: every email costs money to send and process, and discount codes given to customers who would have bought anyway directly reduce profit margin. A customer who would have paid full price should not receive a discount.

There are three types of customer when it comes to promotional emails:

Target These
Persuadables
Customers who convert because of the email. These are the only ones who generate true incremental revenue.
Avoid — Wasted Discount
Sure Things
Customers who would have bought anyway. Sending them a discount just reduces your margin for free.
Avoid — No Return
Lost Causes
Customers who will not convert regardless of any communication. The email cost is wasted entirely.

This analysis addresses both questions: first, whether the campaign worked on average, and second, how to identify which specific customers fall into the persuadable group.

📊 The Business Stakes

With an average order value of £50 and an email cost of £0.50 per contact, the ROI of a well-targeted campaign is dramatically higher than a blanket send. Our analysis shows that targeting the right 30% of customers achieves a far better return than contacting everyone — while spending a fraction of the budget. The difference between smart and indiscriminate targeting is not marginal; it can determine whether a campaign is profitable at all.

Section 04

Was the Experiment Set Up Correctly?

Before drawing any conclusions from the data, we must confirm that the experiment itself was properly designed. If the randomisation was flawed — for example, if older customers were more likely to end up in the treatment group — then any difference in conversion rates could be explained by age, not the email. This would make the results meaningless.

Sample Ratio Mismatch (SRM) Check

The first check is whether the two groups are roughly equal in size. A badly implemented experiment might accidentally assign far more people to one arm than the other. We use a statistical test (chi-squared) to confirm the 50/50 split was achieved correctly.

✅ No Sample Ratio Mismatch detected. Control: 2,501 customers (50.0%), Treatment: 2,499 customers (50.0%). The split is statistically indistinguishable from a perfect 50/50 assignment. The randomisation machinery worked correctly.

Are the Two Groups Comparable?

Even with a correct size split, we need to check that the two groups are similar on every characteristic we measured before the email was sent. If one group happened to be younger, wealthier, or more loyal than the other, any conversion difference could be down to those factors rather than the email itself.

We ran a battery of statistical tests across all pre-experiment customer characteristics:

CharacteristicTest UsedResultVerdict
AgeKS test (continuous) p > 0.05 ✓ Balanced
Loyalty ScoreKS test (continuous) p > 0.05 ✓ Balanced
Previous PurchasesKS test (continuous) p > 0.05 ✓ Balanced
GenderChi-squared test p > 0.05 ✓ Balanced
Income BracketChi-squared test p > 0.05 ✓ Balanced

All five checks passed. The two groups are statistically indistinguishable on every measured characteristic before the email was sent. This is the experimental foundation that allows us to attribute any difference in conversions solely to the email campaign.

Conversion rate tells us whether customers buy; spend tells us how much they spend. Because most customers spend £0 (they did not convert), the spend distribution is heavily skewed — a small number of converters drive all the revenue. We confirmed that the treatment group also showed higher spend using a non-parametric statistical test appropriate for skewed data (Mann-Whitney U). The email campaign increased both the likelihood of purchase and the total revenue generated.

Section 06

The A/B Test — Did the Campaign Work?

The exploratory analysis showed a clear visual difference between the two groups. But visual differences can sometimes be coincidences, especially in smaller datasets. Formal hypothesis testing tells us: what is the probability that a difference this large could have happened by chance even if the email had no real effect?

How Statistical Testing Works — Without the Jargon

Imagine flipping a coin 2,500 times and getting 530 heads instead of the expected 500. Is the coin unfair, or did you just get lucky? Statistical tests calculate exactly this probability. In our case, we ask: if the email truly had zero effect, how likely is it that the treatment group would convert 5.45 percentage points higher than control purely by luck?

✅ Result: Highly Statistically Significant. The probability of observing a difference this large by chance — if the email had no real effect — is less than 0.1% (p < 0.001). By any conventional scientific standard, we can conclude the email campaign caused a genuine increase in conversions.

What Is a Confidence Interval?

The confidence intervals tell us the plausible range for the true conversion rate. We are 95% confident that the control group's true conversion rate lies between 6.81% and 8.91%, and the treatment group's between 11.97% and 14.63%. Crucially, these two intervals do not overlap at all — further confirming that the difference is real.

Subgroup Analysis — Does the Effect Hold Everywhere?

We also tested whether the campaign effect holds within specific customer segments — not just on average. Across gender, income bracket, age groups, and loyalty tiers, the direction of the effect (email group converts more) is consistent in every segment. The size of the effect varies — with older and higher-loyalty customers seeing larger lifts — but there is no segment where the email appears harmful or counterproductive.

How Large an Effect Can We Detect in Future Experiments?

Power analysis tells us how large an experiment needs to be to reliably detect a given effect size. With approximately 1,500 customers per arm, a future experiment could reliably detect a difference as small as 3 percentage points. This is useful for planning targeted sub-segment tests — for example, testing a different email design for the high-loyalty segment specifically.

Section 07

Uplift Modelling — Who Responds Best?

The A/B test has answered whether the campaign works. Now we need to answer for whom. This is where uplift modelling comes in — a more advanced technique that goes beyond average effects to estimate the impact of the email on each individual customer.

Why Average Effects Are Not Enough

The 5.45 percentage point average lift is a single number that hides enormous variation. Some customers respond dramatically to the email; others barely respond at all. If we send to everyone, we pay to contact the non-responders. If we could identify and target only the likely responders, we could achieve the same (or better) conversion volume at a fraction of the cost.

🎯 The Uplift Modelling Goal

For each customer, estimate: "By how many percentage points does this individual's probability of buying increase if they receive the email?" This number is called the Individual Treatment Effect (ITE). Customers with high ITEs are the persuadables we want to target. Customers with ITEs near zero are sure things or lost causes — a waste of budget.

How We Built the Models

We trained four separate machine learning models, each taking a different technical approach to estimating individual treatment effects. All use customer characteristics (age, loyalty score, income, past purchases, gender) as inputs.

ModelApproach in Plain English
T-Learner (Logistic Regression) Builds two separate simple models — one for the email group, one for the no-email group — and subtracts the predictions to estimate the email's effect.
T-Learner (Random Forest) Same two-model approach, but using a more complex algorithm that can detect non-linear patterns (e.g. "the email works especially well for customers over 60 with high loyalty").
T-Learner (Gradient Boosting) Same two-model approach using gradient boosting — an algorithm that iteratively improves its predictions, often the most powerful for structured data.
S-Learner (Gradient Boosting) A single model that is given the email/no-email indicator as an additional input, then scored twice (once as "received email", once as "no email") to estimate the difference.

Which Model Performed Best?

We evaluated all four models on a held-out test set — customers the models had never seen during training. The key metric is the Qini coefficient: a measure of how well the model ranks customers from most to least responsive. A Qini of 0 means the model is no better than random; higher is better.

🏆 Best Model: T-Learner with Logistic Regression. Despite being the simplest of the four models, it achieved the highest Qini coefficient on the test set — meaning it is the most accurate at ranking customers by their responsiveness to the email. This is not unusual: when the underlying relationship between customer characteristics and response is relatively straightforward, simpler models often outperform complex ones.

Does the Ranking Actually Work? — The Decile Table

The ultimate proof of a good uplift model is whether the customers it ranks as "most responsive" genuinely convert at higher rates when emailed. We split the test set into 10 equal groups (deciles) ranked from lowest predicted responsiveness (Decile 1) to highest (Decile 10), and measured what actually happened:

Section 08

Business Targeting — Where to Spend the Budget

With a validated uplift model in hand, we can now answer the most practically important question: exactly which customers should receive the email to maximise return on investment?

The Economics of Targeting

For this analysis, we use the following assumptions:

  • Cost per email sent: £0.50
  • Average order value: £50
  • Target population: ranked in descending order by predicted uplift score

ROI is calculated as: (incremental revenue from conversions caused by the email) ÷ (total email send cost). A customer who converts because of the email generates £50 revenue at a cost of £0.50 — a 100× return. But customers who would have converted anyway, or who will not convert regardless, generate no incremental revenue while still costing £0.50.

Optimal Targeting Threshold

By sweeping through different targeting fractions — from sending to just 5% of customers to sending to 100% — we can identify the threshold that maximises ROI. The ROI curve starts high (top customers are very responsive), peaks at an optimal point, then declines as we add more and more low-responsiveness customers who do not justify the email cost.

✅ Optimal Targeting: The model identifies an optimal targeting threshold at approximately the top 30–40% of customers by predicted uplift score. Targeting beyond this fraction adds customers whose expected incremental conversions do not cover the cost of contacting them, reducing overall ROI. Targeting below this fraction leaves high-value persuadables untouched.

The Three-Segment Framework

For practical campaign execution, we recommend classifying customers into three tiers based on their predicted uplift score:

Top 30% — Always Target
Persuadables

What Customer Characteristics Predict Responsiveness?

We also examined which customer features the model weighs most heavily when predicting uplift. The most important predictors of email responsiveness — in order of influence — are:

  • Loyalty score: The strongest single predictor. Higher-loyalty customers tend to be more responsive to personalised outreach.
  • Age: Older customers (particularly the 61–70 bracket) show above-average responsiveness. Younger customers tend to convert at similar rates regardless of whether they received an email.
  • Previous purchases: Customers with a buying history are more likely to respond to discount incentives than those who have never purchased.
  • Income bracket: Medium and high income customers respond slightly more strongly, possibly because the discount represents a meaningful but not desperate incentive at their income level.
💡 Practical Implication

If the full uplift scoring pipeline cannot be deployed operationally, a simple rule-of-thumb targeting strategy — for example, prioritising customers with loyalty score > 60 and age > 45 — would capture a large share of the persuadable segment at minimal analytical overhead. This is not as precise as the full model, but it outperforms indiscriminate sending considerably.

Section 09

Recommendations and Next Steps

Immediate Actions

  • Roll out the email campaign — the effect is large, real, and statistically unambiguous. A 69.9% relative lift in conversions is a compelling business case for continued investment.
  • Deploy uplift-score-based targeting for the next campaign send. Target the top 30% by predicted responsiveness score; do not contact the bottom 30%.
  • Focus personalisation budget on high-loyalty, older-age segments — they show the strongest response and represent the clearest return on personalisation investment.

Short-Term Improvements

  • Run a follow-up experiment to validate the targeting strategy: send the email only to the top 30% predicted by the uplift model, and confirm that the ROI exceeds the current blanket-send approach in a live test.
  • Test email content variations — the current experiment only tests whether to send an email, not which email to send. A separate A/B test of email subject lines, offer sizes, or personalisation depth could further lift response rates within the persuadable segment.
  • Implement an ongoing holdback: always retain a small random control group (5–10%) in every campaign send. This allows continuous measurement of the campaign's true incremental effect as customer behaviour evolves over time.

Longer-Term Strategic Recommendations

  • Enrich the customer feature set — collecting additional data points such as browsing behaviour, time since last visit, and product category affinity would substantially improve uplift model accuracy.
  • Introduce CUPED (Controlled-experiment Using Pre-Experiment Data) for future experiments — this technique uses pre-experiment data to reduce statistical noise and allows smaller experiments to detect the same effect size with fewer customers.
  • Build a production uplift scoring pipeline — so that every customer receives an uplift score automatically at campaign planning time, without requiring a manual modelling exercise for each new campaign.
  • Explore doubly robust estimation methods for more statistically rigorous ITE estimates, especially as the feature set grows and models become more complex.
✅ The Value of This Analysis: This project does more than confirm the campaign works. It establishes the analytical foundation — randomisation validation, rigorous hypothesis testing, and individual-level uplift modelling — that allows the business to move from "does email marketing work?" to "exactly who should we email, and when?" That shift in question is worth far more than any single campaign.
